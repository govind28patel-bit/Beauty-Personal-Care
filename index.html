01The problem

Global apps were built for someone else’s shelf.

Indian consumers with diagnosed skin allergies build routines by mixing dermatologist-prescribed actives, Ayurvedic remedies and unbranded local products. Many have no barcode, no standard ingredient list, or labels in Hindi or a regional language.

No existing tool checks that full, mixed shelf against a person’s actual allergy profile, catches allergens hidden under alternate names, or accounts for the higher risk of pigmentation and scarring on melanin-rich skin.

Problem statementIndian adults with sensitive or allergy-prone skin, about 1 in 3, can’t tell whether a new product will react with their skin. Indian labels don’t have to use standard (INCI) ingredient names, and no tool checks their whole mixed shelf against their own allergy profile. So they react even after reading the label.

How might we help them know, before they buy or apply, whether a product is safe for their skin and why, without needing a dermatologist for every decision?

Competitive landscape

Ingredient apps already exist. The gap is the top-right corner: a personal check of the whole routine, built for Indian labels and skin.

Yuka Global scanner

Strength: scale, with 65 million+ users in 12 countries. Gap: rates a product in general, not against your own allergies or Indian ingredient names.

INCIDecoder Reference

Strength: clear ingredient-by-ingredient explanations. Gap: a dictionary to read, not a personal check.

SkinSort Routine tools

Strength: checks how ingredients in a routine interact. Gap: built on a global product catalogue, with no focus on Ayurvedic or regional names.

CureSkin India, AI + doctors

Strength: India-native AI skin analysis with dermatologist follow-ups, acquired in 2024. Gap: sells you a regimen and doesn’t check what’s already on your shelf.

04Business opportunity

A trust product with a B2B engine.

Ingredient lookups are free elsewhere, so a consumer subscription alone won’t carry the business. The asset is the trusted allergy profile. Once people trust DermaSafe with it, dermatologists and clinics have a reason to plug in.

Opportunity statementFor the 1 in 3 Indian skincare buyers with sensitive skin, DermaSafe is the only check that reads Indian labels against your own allergy profile. It earns from people who want ongoing protection and from clinics that want to serve them better. It never earns from brands paying for scores.

The moat: a map of Indian ingredient names

Anyone can copy the screens. What’s hard to copy is a dermatologist-verified map of how one allergen appears on Indian labels: in English, Hindi or regional scripts, Ayurvedic names and misspellings.

Every label photo a user confirms adds to that map. The more people check, the more accurate the next check becomes.

10Trust by design

A warning is only useful if you believe it.

An app that cries wolf gets ignored, and an ignored warning protects no one. So every decision on the risk screens is about earning belief, not raising alarm.

Every flag names its source

Verified source, clinically reviewed rule, or AI interpretation. Separate labels, never merged into one badge.

Calm language

“We found something worth checking”, never “dangerous”. Each flag says it’s a product-safety signal, not a diagnosis.

A human when it’s uncertain

Low confidence shows a score range and routes to a dermatologist instead of a confident-looking guess.

“Flag as wrong”

Every warning can be disputed and sent for human review, so mistakes become corrections.

Consent before data

Allergy data is never sold. Research sharing is off by default, and skin photos need consent before they’re shared.

Free where it counts

The free-versus-paid split lives only in Settings. Sponsored products are labelled and never ranked.

12Decisions from critique

What review changed.

Faculty review and a heuristic audit of the first version exposed places where the design worked against its own principles. These changes are in the prototype.

AppliedSmaller, labelled navigation

Faculty feedback: the floating bar was too large. Its 74 px circles took about 12% of the screen and covered primary buttons. The docked bar names every tab, and it hides during the seven-step check so people finish the task.

First version
Prototype
HomeHistoryCheckDoctorsProfile

AppliedConfidence as levels

“89%” suggested statistical precision the model doesn’t have. The prototype shows “High” with the four checks behind it, including the one that’s weak.

AppliedVerification describes data

A “Verified Safe Formula” badge sat above “2 flagged”, so the screen said safe and unsafe at once. Now each field shows its source, with a plain explainer of what verified does and doesn’t mean.

AppliedThe next step is never hidden

Main buttons used to sit under the navigation. Every screen now keeps its main action clear, and uncertain results get their own screen with a range and a route to a doctor.

15Reflection

Trust is the feature.

The hardest design problem wasn’t scanning or scoring. It was making a warning people believe without making them afraid. Separating sources, keeping language calm and giving every result a human escape route did more for trust than any visual polish.

Next, I want to test the OCR flow on real regional-language labels and measure the allergen catch rate with a dermatologist-reviewed test set.
